# Grid position wins; structure beats features

Almost nobody who has measured honestly beats the starting grid by much, so tying a grid-top-3 baseline is a normal result, not a failure. The best-documented hobby project uses a walk-forward backtest over 190 races. It improves log loss over a grid-history baseline by **0.249 ± 0.099**, but its top-1 accuracy rises only from **55.3% to 60.0%**. An earlier version of the same model lost to "the pole-sitter wins" ([f1-prediction-platform](https://f1-prediction-platform-web.pages.dev/docs/prediction-model)). A university student project found that **the grid P1–3 baseline beat its model outright** ([GWU](https://datasci.columbian.gwu.edu/sites/g/files/zaxdzs4746/files/2023-02/f1-final-presentation.pdf)). Academic statisticians use joint ranking models (Plackett-Luce / rank-ordered logit) with driver and constructor strengths. They score these with log scores and LOO-CV, not hit rates against the grid, and they find that the car explains about **88%** of the variance in results ([van Kesteren & Bergkamp](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/abs/2203.08489)). Industry offers no public pre-race benchmark. AWS F1 Insights predicts live, in-race events ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/accelerating-innovation-how-serverless-machine-learning-on-aws-powers-f1-insights)), and no study of how well F1 betting odds are calibrated turned up. For our project, the most promising changes are structural. First, model each race jointly so the 20 probabilities are consistent, for example with a conditional logit / Plackett-Luce or by renormalising so podium probabilities sum to 3. Second, add a grid × track-overtaking-difficulty interaction and a reliability prior. Third, judge progress mainly on Brier and log loss, because a hit-count gain over ~2.25/3 will be small and noisy on one season.

## Hobbyist "85–91% accuracy" claims hide a trivial baseline

Most open-source projects follow the same recipe: FastF1, Ergast or OpenF1 data, qualifying position plus rolling form, and a random forest or XGBoost. Few report a grid baseline. One RandomForest project uses a **random 80/20 `train_test_split`** across 2014–2024, which leaks future information, and reports "65% podium" with no baseline ([Ndungu](https://frank-ndungu.vercel.app/blog/f1-prediction-models)). Another uses the same time split as ours (train 2023–24, test 2025). It reports **91.0% podium accuracy and AUC 0.967**, with no numeric grid comparison and a season-average team feature that may include future races ([Rhianne G](https://rhianneg.github.io/projects/project1_Race-Predictor/race-predictor.html)). The most widely covered repo (~560 stars) regresses lap times and ranks drivers by them, with no backtest at all ([Techstrong](https://techstrong.ai/ai-at-the-edge/data-scientist-uses-ai-to-predict-f1-winners); [Motorsport.com](https://www.motorsport.com/f1/news/fans-are-using-ai-to-predict-f1-race-results-and-the-software-is-only-getting-smarter/10717037/)). Per-driver binary accuracy is misleading here: predicting "no podium" for all 20 drivers is already right for 17 of them, or 85%. None of these numbers can be compared with our hits-per-race metric. A LightGBM project that used TimeSeriesSplit reported a much more modest **~56.7%** CV podium accuracy, with wide confidence intervals ([Box Box](https://moukthika-gunapaneedu.github.io/box-box/model/)).

The exception is the f1-prediction-platform's weighted-v4 model, which is worth studying closely. It is refit before every race and has seven standardised features. The largest weights are grid as −log(slot) (**+0.64**), team race pace (**+0.57**), driver points (**+0.56**) and a **grid × (1 − circuit overtake rate)** "processional" term (**+0.52**). Teammate edge is small (+0.14). Scores are normalised into a softmax over each race, and the model reaches log loss **1.208 vs 1.457** for the grid-history baseline. Winner-in-top-3 rises only from 87.9% to 90.0% ([f1-prediction-platform](https://f1-prediction-platform-web.pages.dev/docs/prediction-model)). The gain shows up mostly in probability quality, not in picking different drivers. Our result already points the same way.

## Academics rank the whole field and separate car from driver

The peer-reviewed literature treats a race as a single ranking rather than 20 independent yes/no outcomes. In the rank-ordered logit / Plackett-Luce model, the finishing order is built by repeatedly picking the next finisher with a softmax over the remaining drivers' strengths. Van Kesteren & Bergkamp fit a Bayesian multilevel version on 2014–2021, splitting ability into long-run and seasonal driver and constructor effects. Their estimated variances are constructor advantage **2.65**, constructor form 0.54, driver skill **0.29** and driver form 0.12 ([arXiv 2203.08489](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/abs/2203.08489)). An earlier 1950–2014 multilevel study put the team share at about **86%** ([Bell et al.](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/96995/14/WRRO_96995.pdf)). Henderson & Kirrane compare truncated (top-k only) and time-weighted Plackett-Luce models specifically for *forecasting* ([Project Euclid](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/bayesian-analysis/volume-13/issue-2/A-Comparison-of-Truncated-and-Time-Weighted-PlackettLuce-Models-for/10.1214/17-BA1048.full)). Powell's generalised Elo turns Plackett-Luce into an online estimator that updates after each race ([White Rose](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/200471)). Martin Ingram's blog model adds a separate Bernoulli DNF process to a rank-ordered logit ([blog](https://martiningram.github.io/f1-model)).

These papers come with two caveats for us. First, van Kesteren & Bergkamp state that their model is descriptive and not suitable for forecasting, because season effects are fitted on the full season and so contain future races. That is exactly the leakage our CLAUDE.md rules prohibit, so only online or time-weighted variants carry over. Second, the papers evaluate with ELPD and posterior checks. Adding circuit and weather terms changed ELPD by amounts within standard error (−3993.36 basic vs −3992.29 circuit) ([arXiv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/abs/2203.08489)). None reports a figure for how much it beats qualifying order. On the value of grid position itself, pole is worth about **two finishing places, or +10 percentage points of win probability** ([Wesselbaum & Owen](https://au.vlex.com/vid/the-value-of-pole-862899004)). In an ordinal model covering 2006–2023, the qualifying coefficient (**1.558**) is about four times that of any practice session (FP2 0.368). Over 2010–23 the grid-to-finish Spearman correlation is **0.748** ([arXiv 2507.10966](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.10966v1)).

## Markets and teams offer methods, not benchmarks

AWS F1 Insights runs XGBoost models trained on historical timing, tyre and weather data, served on live timing. They predict gaps, overtake probability and undercut threats during the race, and AWS publishes no accuracy figures ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/accelerating-innovation-how-serverless-machine-learning-on-aws-powers-f1-insights)). Teams build strategy on Monte Carlo race simulators with random accidents, failures and safety cars ([Heilmeier et al.](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/10/12/4229/html)). Neither is a pre-race podium forecaster we can compare against.

Betting markets would be the strongest benchmark, because post-qualifying podium odds already price in grid, pace, reliability and weather. Formula1.com publishes odds from F1's official data supplier ([Formula1.com](https://www.formula1.com/en/latest/article/betting-guide-who-are-the-favourites-as-f1-moves-on-to-hungary.MDxTg5ThdAlyver4kIe21)). However, no F1-specific study of market efficiency or calibration was found. The general evidence comes from horse racing, where favourites lose ~5.5% and 100/1+ longshots ~61% ([Snowberg & Wolfers](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w15923/w15923.pdf)). Odds need de-vigging, for example with the Shin or power methods ([Wikipedia](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Favourite-longshot_bias)), and with ~24 races a season, small samples make it hard to tell real efficiency from apparent efficiency ([Bielefeld](https://pub.uni-bielefeld.de/record/2983980)). An odds baseline would mean collecting historical odds by hand. It is a stretch goal, not a requirement.

## Where our model can realistically gain

Grid position already contains most of the car and driver signal, so new features only help if they say something the grid does not. The evidence points to four candidates, roughly in order of promise.

**Joint per-race modelling.** Independent logistic regression can give four drivers 0.6 each, or a race total of 2.1. A conditional logit (softmax over the 20 drivers in each race) fixes this, as does renormalising predictions so they sum to 3. P(podium) can then come from Plackett-Luce Monte Carlo sampling. This is the main methodological difference between our model and both the academic models and the only hobby model that beats the grid, and it should improve Brier directly.

**Track grid-stickiness × grid.** Pole conversion varies widely by circuit: Monaco **71.4%** since 2004 ([GPFans](https://www.gpfans.com/en/f1-news/1085387/f1-monaco-grand-prix-wins-from-pole-position-alltime-stats/)) against 17 of 40 at Interlagos ([Lights Out](https://lightsoutblog.com/f1-grid-slot-stats-sao-paulo-grand-prix-interlagos)). In v4 this interaction is the second-largest weight. To stay leakage-safe, compute the index only from *earlier* races at that track, for example the grid-to-finish correlation over prior seasons.

**Team race pace vs qualifying pace.** Team race pace is v4's second-strongest feature, and some cars race better than they qualify. A rolling (shift(1)) "race finish minus grid" per team captures this without practice data. FP2 long runs are a weaker option: v4 tried them and dropped them as noise ([f1-prediction-platform](https://f1-prediction-platform-web.pages.dev/docs/prediction-model)). Fuel loads are unknown, and FastF1's own degradation example applies no fuel correction ([FastF1 guide](https://mintlify.com/theOehrly/Fast-F1/guides/data-analysis)).

**Reliability / DNF prior.** Following Ingram, a rolling team DNF rate, or a two-part model P(finish) × P(podium | finish), spreads probability more sensibly. DNFs and first-lap incidents remain largely unpredictable.

On the other side, weather extensions did not improve the academic fits, with only 17 wet races out of 160 ([arXiv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/abs/2203.08489)), and race-day weather is leakage unless it comes from a forecast. The car's ~88% share also means driver form features add little beyond what team form and teammate comparison already capture.

## Conclusion

A model tying the grid on hits per race while posting a Brier of ~0.059 is in line with the most rigorous public work, which beats the grid mainly in probability quality, not in different picks. The honest goal is therefore to beat grid on Brier and log loss while staying level or slightly ahead on hits, with the 2024 validation results reported with uncertainty. A gain of 0.1 hits per race over ~22 races is hard to tell apart from noise. The strongest next step is a joint per-race model, not more features. If that helps, the grid × track-stickiness interaction and a reliability prior are the best-supported additions. Two gaps remain open and are themselves a portfolio contribution: no public project reports podium hits per race against a grid-top-3 baseline in the 2022+ era, and none compares a joint ranking model with independent classifiers for podiums. Doing both, cleanly time-split, would put this project ahead of nearly all published hobby work.
